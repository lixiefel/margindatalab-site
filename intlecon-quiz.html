PART A - INTERNATIONAL TRADE (Lectures 1-12)

LECTURE 1 - World trade: an overview and the gravity model

Question 1
In the gravity model Tij = A × (Yi)a × (Yj)b / (Dij)c, suppose a = b = c = 1. If the GDPs of both countries double and the distance between them also doubles, predicted trade between the two countries will:
Answer: D — Numerator rises by 2 × 2 = 4, denominator by 2, so trade rises by a factor 4/2 = 2.
Question 2
According to the typical estimates discussed in the lecture, a 1% increase in the distance between two countries is associated with a fall in their bilateral trade of roughly:
Answer: C — Lecture 1: distance elasticity c is typically 0.7-1; size elasticities a and b are about 1.
Question 3
In the gravity model, the term Dij (trade impediments between i and j) can capture all of the following EXCEPT:
Answer: A — Economic size enters through Yi and Yj, not through Dij. Language, tariffs, borders, culture and geography are all trade impediments.
Question 4
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: If Canada and the US trade much more with each other than the gravity model predicts from their GDPs and distance, this proves that NAFTA caused the extra trade.
Statement II: Comparing actual trade flows with gravity-model predictions can be used to detect 'trade anomalies' and to study the effect of factors such as trade agreements or a common language.
Answer: C — Extra trade is consistent with a NAFTA effect but does not prove it; other factors (e.g. a long shared border, language, culture) may explain it. II is how the lecture uses the model.
Question 5
World trade grew rapidly between 1870 and 1913 and then collapsed because of WWI, the Great Depression, protectionism and WWII. According to the lecture, world trade (relative to output) returned to its pre-WWI level only around:
Answer: C — Trade only regained pre-WWI levels around 1970; since then it has kept rising (end of the USSR, China opening up).
Question 6
Which statement about the current composition of world trade is correct according to the lecture?
Answer: A — Today roughly 73% merchandise / 27% services, manufactures dominate, and intermediate goods are more than 50% of world exports. Most services are still non-tradable.
Question 7
The lecture concluded that 'the world is not yet flat'. Which observation best supports this conclusion?
Answer: C — 'Not flat' = distance still matters a lot even after big technological falls in trade costs.
Question 8
Based on the table, which statement is correct?
Answer: B — Home needs fewer hours for both goods (absolute advantage in both). Opportunity cost of cheese: Home 2/3 wine, Foreign 8/4 = 2 wine, so Home has the CA in cheese and Foreign in wine.
Question 9
Home's production possibility frontier can be written as:
Answer: B — 2 QC + 3 QW = 1,200, so QW = 1,200/3 - (2/3) QC = 400 - (2/3) QC.
Question 10
If trade leads both countries to specialize completely, the world relative price of cheese PC/PW must lie:
Answer: D — The world price lies between the two autarky prices = opportunity costs: Home 2/3, Foreign 2.
Question 11
For relative prices strictly between the two autarky relative prices, the world relative supply curve of cheese, (QC + Q*C)/(QW + Q*W), is:
Answer: B — Both countries fully specialize: Home makes 1,200/2 = 600 cheese, Foreign makes 1,600/4 = 400 wine, so RS = 600/400 = 1.5 (vertical segment).
Question 12
Suppose the world relative price of cheese settles at PC/PW = 1. Home's wage relative to Foreign's wage (w/w*) then equals:
Answer: D — w = PC/aLC = P/2 and w* = PW/a*LW = P/4, so w/w* = 2 (within the bounds a*LW/aLW = 4/3 and a*LC/aLC = 4).
Question 13
At the world relative price PC/PW = 1, one hour of Home labor buys:
Answer: A — With trade: 1 hour = 1/2 kg cheese, exchanged 1:1 for 1/2 litre wine. In autarky: 1 hour makes 1/aLW = 1/3 litre wine. Real wage in terms of wine rises.
Question 14
In a Ricardian economy, if the relative price of cheese PC/PW is higher than aLC/aLW, then:
Answer: B — Zero profits: wC = PC/aLC and wW = PW/aLW. wC > wW exactly when PC/PW > aLC/aLW, so all workers move to cheese.
Question 15
If the relative wage w/w* equals 2.5, then:
Answer: B — Home produces good i if w/w* < a*Li/aLi. Ratios: 10, 5, 3, 2, 1. Only apples, bananas and caviar have a ratio above 2.5.
Question 16
Starting from w/w* = 2.5, suppose Home's labor force grows relative to Foreign's. A plausible consequence is:
Answer: C — Higher relative labor supply L/L* moves along the downward-sloping relative labor demand curve: w/w* falls, so the range of goods made at Home widens.
Question 17
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: A country that is less productive than its trading partner in every good cannot gain from trade in the Ricardian model.
Statement II: In the Ricardian model, low wages in a less productive country offset its low productivity, giving it a cost advantage in its export good.
Answer: C — Gains depend on comparative, not absolute, advantage (misconception 1). II: high productivity offsets high wages and low wages offset low productivity.
Question 18
Which of the following facts can the simple one-factor Ricardian model NOT explain?
Answer: A — With only one factor, everybody gains in the Ricardian model; income distribution effects need the specific factors and HO models.
Question 19
Costinot and Donaldson (2012) tested the Ricardian model by:
Answer: C — They used soil/land productivity estimates; predicted crop patterns correlate strongly with actual ones. (Korean wages actually rose with productivity.)
Question 20
The real-world economy shows much less specialization than the Ricardian model predicts. According to the lecture, which of the following is NOT a reason for this?
Answer: D — Having only one factor is an assumption of the model that drives its extreme specialization; the other three are real-world features that limit specialization.

LECTURE 4 - The specific factors model

Question 21
Compared with the Ricardian model, the specific factors model presented in the lecture:
Answer: A — Labor is mobile across sectors, capital (cars) and land (food) are specific. Diminishing MPL makes opportunity costs rising, so the PPF is curved.
Question 22
In the specific factors model (cars made with labor and capital, food with labor and land), the opportunity cost of producing one extra car, in terms of food, equals:
Answer: A — One extra car needs 1/MPLC units of labor, which could have produced MPLF/MPLC units of food.
Question 23
In equilibrium in a specific factors economy, MPLC = 4 and MPLF = 10, and the price of food is PF = 2. The price of cars PC and the wage w are:
Answer: C — w = PF × MPLF = 2 × 10 = 20. Wages are equal across sectors, so 20 = PC × 4, so PC = 5.
Question 24
Suppose the prices of both cars and food rise by 10%. In the specific factors model:
Answer: A — A proportional price change leaves relative prices unchanged: wages rise by the same 10%, allocation and all real incomes are unchanged.
Question 25
Suppose the price of cars rises by 10% while the price of food is unchanged. Fill in the blanks: the nominal wage rises by [1]; capital owners [2]; landowners [3]; the effect on workers is [4].
Answer: A — Labor moves into cars, lowering MPLC, so w rises by less than PC. Workers buy more food but fewer cars, so the effect depends on preferences.
Question 26
Country Z exports wheat (produced with labor and land) and imports textiles (produced with labor and capital). According to the specific factors model, opening Z to trade will:
Answer: C — The factor specific to the export sector gains, the factor specific to the import- competing sector loses, the mobile factor's change is ambiguous.
Question 27
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: In the specific factors model, a country as a whole gains from trade in the sense that the winners could in principle compensate the losers and still be better off.
Statement II: Because such compensation is possible, in practice everybody in the country gains from trade.
Answer: B — Possible compensation does not mean it actually happens; redistribution is usually hard to implement.
Question 28
Consider an economy producing one good (food) with land and labor, with diminishing MPL. Employment is L and the real wage equals MPL at L. Fill in the blanks: the area under the MPL curve up to L measures [1]; the rectangle (real wage × L) measures [2]; the area between the MPL curve and the wage line measures [3].
Answer: A — Output = integral of MPL. Workers get w × L; the remaining triangle goes to the owners of the specific factor (land).
Question 29
Two countries produce food with identical technology. Home has more workers per unit of land, so its real wage is lower than Foreign's. If workers can migrate freely:
Answer: D — Workers move until real wages are equal. Labor goes where it is more productive, so world output rises (the 'red triangle').
Question 30
According to the lecture, which statement about trade and unemployment in the US is correct?
Answer: A — 1996-2008: only ~2.5% of involuntary displacements were from import competition/offshoring, and the raw correlation is small. A more careful look gives a much gloomier picture.
Question 31
Why are trade restrictions often adopted even though the country as a whole gains from trade?
Answer: C — Lecture 4's first look at political economy: small interest groups (e.g. EU farmers) dominate trade policy.
Question 32
According to the lecture, recent immigration into the US mainly concerned low-skilled workers. This is predicted to have:
Answer: B — More competition for low-skilled jobs lowers their real wages; goods made by low- skilled workers get cheaper, raising real wages of the more educated.
Question 33
Country A has 100 workers and 50 machines. Country B has 300 workers and 200 machines. According to the definition used in the HO model:
Answer: A — Abundance is relative: L/K = 2 in A and 1.5 in B, so A is labor abundant (L/K > L*/K*), even though B has more workers.
Question 34
In the HO model, car production is labor-intensive relative to food production if:
Answer: B — Factor intensity is defined at ANY given factor prices; wages are equal across sectors because labor is mobile.
Question 35
Cars are labor-intensive. An increase in the relative price of cars PC/PF leads to: w/r [1]; the labor/capital ratio in both sectors [2]; the real wage [3]; the real rental rate [4].
Answer: C — Shifting to cars creates excess demand for labor, so w/r rises; firms economize on labor (L/K falls in both sectors), so MPL rises and MPK falls in both sectors: real wage up, real rental down.
Question 36
'If the relative price of a good increases, the real return of the factor used intensively in that good increases and the real return of the other factor decreases.' This is the:
Answer: B — Rybczynski = endowment changes at given prices; HO theorem = trade pattern; FPE = factor prices converge with trade.
Question 37
Holding goods prices constant, an increase in a country's labor force (cars are labor- intensive) leads to:
Answer: A — Rybczynski theorem. With prices fixed, w/r and input mixes are fixed; capital and labor both move into cars to absorb the extra workers, so food output falls.
Question 38
At the current production point, the opportunity cost of one car is 3 units of food, while the market relative price is PC/PF = 4. To reach the highest isovalue line, the economy should:
Answer: D — Selling a car yields 4 food on the market but costs only 3 food in production, so shifting toward cars raises the value of output until the PPF slope equals the price ratio.
Question 39
Home (labor abundant) and Foreign (capital abundant) have identical technologies and preferences; cars are labor-intensive. In autarky:
Answer: B — Home supplies relatively more cars at any price, so cars are relatively cheap at Home; via the goods-price/factor-price link, a low PC/PF goes with a low w/r.
Question 40
In the same two-country HO world, opening to trade:
Answer: C — In each country the relative price of the good using its abundant factor intensively rises, so the abundant factor gains and the scarce factor loses (Stolper-Samuelson).
Question 41
Factor price equalization (FPE) may fail to occur for all of the following reasons EXCEPT:
Answer: D — Both countries producing both goods is a condition FOR FPE; incomplete specialization is needed. The other three break goods-price or factor-price equalization.
Question 42
The 'Leontief paradox' (1953) refers to the finding that:
Answer: D — This contradicts the HO prediction. Evidence fits better once skill endowments are considered.
Question 43
According to the lecture, the empirical evidence on the HO model is:
Answer: B — Global factor-content tests perform poorly; North-South trade (and Asia's rising skill-intensive exports to the US) fit well; FPE evidence is particularly bad.
Question 44
Which observation suggests that skill-biased technological change, rather than trade, was the main driver of the rising US skill premium?
Answer: D — HO predicts that a higher relative skilled wage makes both sectors use FEWER skilled workers per unskilled worker. The opposite happened, consistent with SBTC (also: the skill premium rose in skill-scarce countries too).
Question 45
Which is a key difference between the Heckscher-Ohlin model and the Ricardian model?
Answer: B — Ricardo: technology differences and complete specialization. HO: same technology, two mobile factors, both goods produced, winners and losers.

LECTURE 7 - The standard trade model

Question 46
Home exports cars and imports food. Home's terms of trade are:
Answer: A — ToT = price of exports / price of imports. The last option equals PF/PC (from the budget constraint), i.e. Foreign's ToT.
Question 47
Home's relative supply curve of cars lies to the right of Foreign's at every relative price, and preferences are identical. Then:
Answer: A — World RS is a weighted average of the two (see appendix proof), so trade makes relative prices converge.
Question 48
Home exports cars. The relative price of cars rises further. Which of the following is certain?
Answer: B — The substitution effect lowers relative car consumption for sure; the income effect (Home is richer, its ToT improved) can raise absolute consumption of both.
Question 49
The gains from international trade can be decomposed into 'gains from exchange' and 'gains from specialization'. The gains from exchange are the gains Home obtains:
Answer: A — Exchange gain: same production, but sell C at a higher world price and buy F at a lower one. Specialization gain: also move production to the optimal Q.
Question 50
Home trades with Foreign. Home experiences export-biased growth. Then:
Answer: C — More world supply of Home's export good lowers its relative price. Growth at home: positive direct effect + negative ToT effect. (Foreign's ToT improve.)
Question 51
Foreign experiences economic growth that is biased toward the good that Home exports. For Home, this growth:
Answer: B — Growth abroad affects Home only through the ToT. More world supply of Home's export good = export-biased growth from Home's perspective, so Home's ToT and welfare fall.
Question 52
According to the standard trade model, economic growth in China can reduce US welfare only if Chinese growth:
Answer: D — Only import-biased growth in China (= export-biased from the US view) worsens US ToT. Empirically, ToT changes for high-income countries have been small.
Question 53
Home (a large country) exports cars and imposes a tariff on food imports. Then:
Answer: A — Inside Home food becomes relatively more expensive: RD for cars rises and RS of cars falls, so the world relative price of cars rises.
Question 54
Home (a large country) exports cars and subsidizes its car exports. In the two-country standard trade model:
Answer: D — World RS of cars rises and world RD falls, so the world car price falls. ToT loss + distortion: welfare falls; Foreign gains.
Question 55
There are many countries. The US and the EU both export agricultural goods. An EU export subsidy on agricultural goods:
Answer: B — An export subsidy lowers the world price of that good, whoever imposes it; that hurts all other exporters of the good.
Question 56
Oilland exports oil and imports manufactured goods. Which event improves Oilland's terms of trade?
Answer: C — ToT = Poil/Pmanuf. Cheaper manufactures raise it. The other three lower the relative price of oil.
Question 57
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: A fall in a country's terms of trade can never reduce its welfare below the level it would have in autarky.
Statement II: An import tariff always lowers the welfare of a large country, because it distorts domestic production and consumption.
Answer: B — I: the export good's relative price stays above its autarky level, otherwise the country would not export it. II: for a large country the ToT gain can outweigh the distortion.

LECTURE 8 - External economies of scale

Question 58
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: External economies of scale are compatible with perfect competition and an industry consisting of many small firms.
Statement II: Under internal economies of scale, a firm's average cost depends on the size of the industry rather than on the size of the firm.
Answer: B — External EoS: AC depends on industry size (many small competing firms). Internal EoS: AC depends on firm size (few large firms, imperfect competition).
Question 59
London attracts highly skilled financial specialists from all over the world, which lets financial firms there respond flexibly to market conditions. This best illustrates which source of external economies of scale?
Answer: A — A large concentrated industry attracts a pool of workers, cutting search costs for workers and hiring costs for firms (self-reinforcing).
Question 60
In an industry with external economies of scale, the industry supply curve is:
Answer: B — The larger the industry, the lower average costs and hence the price competitive firms charge.
Question 61
Two countries produce buttons under external economies of scale. Opening to trade concentrates all button production in one country. Compared with autarky, button prices:
Answer: A — Concentrating production in one location exploits the external EoS fully. Contrast with the standard trade model, where relative prices converge.
Question 62
Vietnam could produce buttons at a lower average cost than China at every level of output, but China has a large established button industry that sells at world price P1. No Vietnamese firm enters because:
Answer: D — Lock-in from a head start: the 'right' country need not end up producing.
Question 63
Switzerland established its watch industry first, although Thailand could produce watches more cheaply at every output level. Thailand imports watches at the world price P1. Under external economies of scale:
Answer: A — If Thailand reverts to autarky its own industry can develop (P2 < P1); this is an argument to protect the industry until it can compete.
Question 64
'Dynamic' increasing returns to scale arise when:
Answer: C — Static external EoS depend on current output; dynamic ones on accumulated experience, which makes history even more important.
Question 65
The table gives each industry's share of employment in the three regions of a country. East Central West Industry W 2% 20% 3% Industry X 10% 11% 10% Industry Y 25% 24% 26% Industry Z 1% 2% 18% Which industries are most likely tradable?
Answer: C — Non-tradables have similar employment shares across regions; tradable production clusters in a few locations.
Question 66
The production function Q = K0.6 × L0.6 exhibits:
Answer: A — Exponents sum to 1.2 > 1, so IRS. MPL = 0.6 K0.6 L-0.4 falls as L rises, so each factor has diminishing returns. Returns to scale differ from returns to one factor.
Question 67
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: External economies of scale can theoretically justify temporarily protecting an industry until it is large or experienced enough to compete on world markets.
Statement II: In practice it is easy for governments to identify which protected industries will become competitive.
Answer: B — Infant-industry logic is theoretically valid, but it is hard to pick winners, and protection can reduce incentives to innovate.

LECTURE 9 - Instruments of trade policy

Question 68
A small country imports bicycles at a world price of 200. The components, also imported, cost 150. The country imposes a 20% ad valorem tariff on bicycles and no tariff on components. The effective rate of protection of domestic bicycle assembly is:
Answer: C — Value added: free trade 200 - 150 = 50; with tariff 240 - 150 = 90. ERP = (90 - 50)/50 = 80% (= t × P/(P - Pintm) = 0.2 × 200/50).
Question 69
Same as the previous question, but the country now also imposes a 10% tariff on the imported components. The effective rate of protection of bicycle assembly becomes:
Answer: C — Value added with tariffs = 240 - 165 = 75; ERP = (75 - 50)/50 = 50%. Taxing inputs reduces the effective protection.
Question 70
Home's import demand curve (MD = D - S) is flatter than Home's demand curve because:
Answer: B — Imports respond through both demand and supply; MD hits the price axis at Home's autarky price.
Question 71
Under free trade, the world price and Home's imports are:
Answer: A — MD = 120 - 20P and XS* = S* - D* = -40 + 20P. Setting them equal: 160 = 40P, so P = 4 and imports = 120 - 80 = 40 (autarky prices: Home 6, Foreign 2).
Question 72
Home imposes a specific tariff of 2 per unit. The new price in Home, the new price in Foreign, and Home's imports are:
Answer: C — PT = P*T + 2. MD: 120 - 20(P* + 2) = 80 - 20P*. Equal to -40 + 20P* gives P* = 3, PT = 5, imports = 20. The Home price rises by only half the tariff (large country).
Question 73
For the tariff in the previous question, the change in Home consumer surplus, the change in Home producer surplus and the government's tariff revenue are, respectively:
Answer: D — Price rises 4 to 5. CS loss = 1 × 70 + 1/2 × 1 × 10 = 75. PS gain = 1 × 40 + 1/2 × 1 × 10 = 45. Revenue = 2 × 20 = 40.
Question 74
The net welfare effect of this tariff for Home is:
Answer: C — -75 + 45 + 40 = +10. Equivalently: ToT gain e = (4 - 3) × 20 = 20, minus efficiency losses b + d = 5 + 5 = 10.
Question 75
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: For a small country, an import tariff always lowers national welfare.
Statement II: For a large country, an import tariff raises the domestic price by less than the amount of the tariff.
Answer: A — Small country: no ToT gain (e = 0), only distortion losses. Large country: the world price falls, so the domestic price rises by less than t.
Question 76
With the quota, the domestic price and the total quota rents are:
Answer: D — Free trade imports = 200 - 100 = 100. Quota: 300 - 20P = 40, so P = 13. Rents = (13 - 10) × 40 = 120.
Question 77
If the import licenses are given away for free to foreign exporters, the country's net welfare loss from the quota is:
Answer: C — CS loss = 3 × 170 + 1/2 × 3 × 30 = 555; PS gain = 3 × 100 + 45 = 345. Loss = 555 - 345 = 210 = distortions (45 + 45 = 90) + rents handed to foreigners (120).
Question 78
A large country introduces a specific export subsidy s. Which statement is correct?
Answer: C — Welfare change = -(b + d + e + f + g): efficiency losses plus a ToT loss, with no possible gain.
Question 79
A local content requirement obliges car assemblers to use 40% domestic parts. Imported parts cost 50 per car, domestically produced parts cost 80. The cost of parts per car after the requirement is:
Answer: B — 0.4 × 80 + 0.6 × 50 = 32 + 30 = 62, i.e. 12 more than with only imported parts. This is typically passed on to consumers.
Question 80
Which pairing of a trade policy with the policy it is equivalent to (in effect) is correct?
Answer: C — Export credit subsidies work like export subsidies; VERs work like import quotas whose rents go to foreigners.
Question 81
Compared with an import tariff that reduces imports by the same amount, a voluntary export restraint (VER):
Answer: A — A VER is a quota imposed by the exporter: the importing country loses the revenue/rents that a tariff would have kept at home.

LECTURE 10 - Trade policy in practice

Question 82
According to the median voter theorem, two competing parties in a democracy should choose trade policies that:
Answer: C — The theorem predicts no quotas, export subsidies or tariffs, yet we see the CAP, Japan's ~1000% rice tariff and the US sugar quota.
Question 83
Why do consumers typically fail to lobby for free trade, while small producer groups successfully lobby for protection?
Answer: D — This is the collective action problem: marginal cost of advocating free trade > marginal benefit per consumer.
Question 84
The table shows payoffs (US, Japan) of trade policy choices. Japan: free trade Japan: protection US: free trade (10, 10) (-10, 20) US: protection (20, -10) (-5, -5) Without a binding international agreement, the predicted outcome is:
Answer: C — Protection is a dominant strategy for each (20 > 10 and -5 > -10), so a prisoner's dilemma results; a binding agreement (WTO, trade agreements) can get both to (10, 10).
Question 85
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: If domestic production generates a marginal social benefit not captured by producers, a tariff can raise welfare if the extra social benefit exceeds the production and consumption distortion losses.
Statement II: Even then, it is in principle better to tackle the market failure directly (e.g. with a production subsidy) than through trade policy.
Answer: A — Lecture: tariff welfare-enhancing if (a + b) < c; but trade policy is only a second- best option, as it is always best to tackle the market failure directly.
Question 86
Which of the following was NOT a reason why import-substituting industrialization (ISI) disappointed?
Answer: D — ISI discouraged exports; it did not cause over-specialization in them. It also involved complex time-consuming regulations.
Question 87
Which statement best summarizes the lecture's evidence on trade liberalization and growth in developing countries?
Answer: C — Asia's take-off cannot be attributed entirely to trade liberalization; high investment and education also mattered.
Question 88
Developing countries often oppose including labor and environmental standards in trade agreements because:
Answer: B — Standards are set by countries themselves; monitoring and consumer information or direct regulation are alternative ways to improve conditions.
Question 89
'Rent seeking', as an argument for free trade, refers to:
Answer: B — Any deviation from free trade can be manipulated by political groups, which wastes resources.
Question 90
A large country can in theory gain from an optimal import tariff. According to the lecture, this argument is doubtful in practice because:
Answer: A — Retaliation can lead to a trade war that leaves everyone worse off.
Question 91
According to the lecture (e.g. the 'China syndrome' research), which statement about import competition and labor-market adjustment in rich countries is correct?
Answer: B — Adjustment is hard (moving, re-training), and losers are difficult to compensate.
Question 92
In an industry, exports are X = 60 and imports are M = 40. The Grubel-Lloyd index GL = 1 - |X - M| / (X + M) equals:
Answer: C — 1 - 20/100 = 0.8, a high degree of intra-industry trade.
Question 93
A Grubel-Lloyd index of 0 for an industry means that:
Answer: A — GL = 0 when X > 0 and M = 0 (or vice versa); GL = 1 when X = M.
Question 94
A monopolist faces demand q = 100 - 2p and has total costs C = 400 + 10q. Its profit- maximizing price and profit are:
Answer: D — Inverse demand p = 50 - q/2, so MR = 50 - q. MR = MC = 10 gives q = 40, p = 30. Profit = 30 × 40 - 400 - 10 × 40 = 400.
Question 95
The equilibrium number of firms n and price p are:
Answer: D — n* = √(S/(bF)) = √(1,000,000/2,500) = 20. p = 2,000 + 10,000/20 = 2,500 (= AC = 20 × 25 + 2,000).
Question 96
This market now integrates with a foreign market with sales of 3,000,000 (same F, c and b). In the integrated market:
Answer: B — S = 4,000,000 so n = √(4,000,000/2,500) = 40 and p = 2,000 + 10,000/40 = 2,250. Output per firm doubles from 50,000 to 100,000.
Question 97
In the monopolistic competition model, trade liberalization (larger market size S) does NOT:
Answer: A — S rises more than n (n rises with the square root of S), so q = S/n rises; consumers gain from more varieties and a lower price.
Question 98
In the (n, p) diagram of the monopolistic competition model, an increase in market size S:
Answer: B — AC = nF/S + c falls for every n when S rises. PP (p = c + 1/(bn)) does not contain S.
Question 99
According to the lecture, trade liberalization in a monopolistically competitive industry with heterogeneous firms:
Answer: D — Firm selection is an additional source of gains from trade.
Question 100
Domestic and foreign markets are symmetric: in each, a firm cannot sell profitably if its marginal cost of serving that market is 50 or higher. Exporting adds a per-unit trade cost of 10. Four firms have marginal costs of 20, 35, 45 and 55. Which firm sells ONLY in its domestic market?
Answer: B — Export requires MC + 10 < 50, i.e. MC < 40: firms with 20 and 35 export. The firm with 45 serves only home; the firm with 55 cannot sell at all.
Question 101
According to the lecture, price discrimination in the form of dumping can occur only if:
Answer: D — Firms must be able to influence prices and goods must not be easily bought in one market and resold in the other.
Question 102
A firm is a monopolist at home and a price-taker abroad at price pfor. Compared with a situation without dumping, when the firm jointly maximizes profits over both markets (dumping):
Answer: C — If MRfor (= pfor) > MRdom, moving units abroad raises profits; extra profit = area FCD in the lecture diagram.
Question 103
Under WTO rules, an anti-dumping tariff should be equal to:
Answer: C — Economists note dumping may benefit domestic consumers, and anti-dumping claims can be a way to obtain protection against more efficient foreign producers.
Question 104
Which of the following is foreign direct investment (FDI)?
Answer: B — FDI requires control: at least 10% of voting shares. Buying an existing firm is brownfield FDI (cross-border M&A); building a new plant is greenfield FDI.
Question 105
A US firm owns a Mexican affiliate that produces components which are shipped to the US parent for final assembly. This is:
Answer: C — Vertical FDI = buyer-seller relationship within the firm (often intermediates); HO- type cost motive.
Question 106
Horizontal FDI is most likely when:
Answer: A — Proximity-concentration trade-off: proximity to customers vs concentrating production to exploit IRS. Also 'tariff-jumping' FDI.
Question 107
A firm decides whether to produce an input in its own foreign affiliate (vertical FDI) or to buy it from an independent foreign supplier (outsourcing). Which consideration favors OUTSOURCING?
Answer: D — The other three are internalization advantages favoring keeping production in-house (the 'I' in Dunning's OLI framework).
Question 108
According to the lecture, how do firms in the same industry typically rank by size and productivity?
Answer: C — Fixed export costs and fixed costs of foreign plants mean only the most productive (largest) firms export, and the very best become multinationals.

PART B - INTERNATIONAL FINANCE (Lectures 13-16)

LECTURE 13 - National income accounting and the balance of payments

Question 109
A country's gross national product (GNP) is:
Answer: B — GNP is by ownership of factors (Dutch-owned factors count as Dutch GNP); only final goods to avoid double counting. The third option defines GDP.
Question 110
GNP = 1,000, depreciation = 100 and net unilateral transfers from abroad = -20. National income is:
Answer: A — National income = GNP - depreciation + net unilateral transfers = 1,000 - 100 - 20 = 880.
Question 111
A country's GNP is 500. Its residents receive 40 from abroad as payment for domestically owned factors (e.g. wages, profits earned abroad), and it pays 25 to foreigners for foreign- owned factors used at home. Its GDP is:
Answer: B — GDP = GNP - payments from abroad for domestic factors + payments to foreign factors = 500 - 40 + 25 = 485.
Question 112
In 2020, Luxembourg's GNP/GDP ratio was about 0.70. According to the lecture, this suggests that:
Answer: D — GNP < GDP means payments to foreign factors exceed receipts from abroad.
Question 113
In an open economy, Y = 2,000, C = 1,200, I = 500 and G = 400. The current account equals:
Answer: B — CA = Y - (C + I + G) = 2,000 - 2,100 = -100: domestic spending exceeds production.
Question 114
Private saving is 300, taxes are 400, government purchases are 450, and investment is 200. The current account is:
Answer: B — Sg = T - G = -50, so S = 300 - 50 = 250. CA = S - I = 250 - 200 = 50.
Question 115
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: Holding private saving and investment constant, a rise in the government deficit reduces the current account one-for-one.
Statement II: If households raise their private saving by exactly the amount of the higher deficit (Ricardian equivalence), the current account does not change.
Answer: A — CA = Sp - (G - T) - I. With Sp constant, a higher deficit lowers the CA; if Sp rises equally, the two effects cancel.
Question 116
A country runs a current account deficit of 50 and its capital account balance is 0. Using the lecture's sign convention (credits +, debits -), its financial account balance is:
Answer: C — CA + FA + KA = 0, so FA = +50: the deficit is financed by selling domestic assets/borrowing abroad.
Question 117
A Dutch resident buys shares of a US company. In the Dutch balance of payments this is recorded as:
Answer: B — Buying foreign assets means the domestic economy gives up money: a debit. Selling domestic assets to foreigners is a credit.
Question 118
Which transaction is recorded in the capital account of the balance of payments?
Answer: A — Capital account: special transfers of non-market, non-produced or intangible assets (debt forgiveness, copyrights, trademarks). Shipping services and wages are current account; bonds are financial account.
Question 119
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: The current account equals national saving minus investment, and can be interpreted as net foreign investment.
Statement II: A current account deficit means the country is lending to the rest of the world.
Answer: B — A CA deficit means the country spends more than it earns, so its net foreign wealth falls: it is borrowing from abroad.
Question 120
Which statement is correct?
Answer: B — Closed: Y = C + I + G so S = I. Open: S = I + CA.
Question 121
According to the lecture's classification, which of the following is an ILLIQUID asset?
Answer: A — Liquid: currency, checking, debit card, savings and time deposits. Illiquid: bonds, loans, foreign-currency deposits, stocks, real estate, art. Illiquid assets earn higher interest.
Question 122
The lecture lists the risk of unexpected inflation as a determinant of INDIVIDUAL money demand, but not of AGGREGATE money demand. Why?
Answer: D — Aggregate money demand Md = P × L(R, Y) depends on R, P and Y.
Question 123
Fill in the blanks: the aggregate real money demand curve L(R, Y) slopes [1] in the interest rate because [2]; an increase in real income Y shifts the curve to the [3].
Answer: B — Interest rates are the opportunity cost of holding cash; higher income means more transactions and hence more demand for money.
Question 124
Suppose real money demand depends on disposable income Y(1 - t), with 0 < t < 1 the income tax rate. If the tax rate t falls, the L-curve in the money market diagram:
Answer: A — Lower t means higher Y(1 - t), so more real money is demanded at every interest rate.
Question 125
In the money market, the interest rate is initially such that money supply exceeds money demand. Then:
Answer: A — With excess supply, holders are only willing to accept a lower interest rate; a lower R raises the quantity of money demanded.
Question 126
The interest rate on euro deposits is 3% and on dollar deposits 5%. Today E$/€ = 1.25 and investors expect E$/€ = 1.30 in one year. The expected dollar rate of return on euro deposits, and the better investment, are:
Answer: D — Expected return = R€ + (Ee - E)/E = 3% + 0.05/1.25 = 3% + 4% = 7% > 5%.
Question 127
R$ = 6% and R€ = 2%. Investors expect E$/€ = 1.04 one year from now. The current exchange rate consistent with interest parity is:
Answer: B — Parity: R$ = R€ + (Ee - E)/E, so (1.04 - E)/E = 0.04 and E = 1.04/1.04 = 1.00. The euro must be expected to appreciate by 4% to compensate for its lower interest rate.
Question 128
In the foreign exchange market diagram, the expected dollar return on euro deposits falls as the current E$/€ rises. Why?
Answer: C — The dollar return R$ does not depend on E (vertical); the euro return R€ + (Ee - E)/E is decreasing in E.
Question 129
Short run, prices fixed, expected future exchange rate given. The US Fed raises the US money supply. Then:
Answer: C — Lower R$ makes euro assets more attractive; investors supply dollars and demand euros.
Question 130
Short run, prices fixed, given expectations. The ECB raises the European money supply. Then:
Answer: B — A foreign money supply change shifts the euro-return curve (left here); R$ is unchanged.
Question 131
Short run, prices fixed, given expectations. US real income rises. Then:
Answer: C — L(R, Y) shifts right, R$ rises, and dollar deposits become more attractive (E$/€ falls).
Question 132
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: In the long run, a permanent 10% increase in a country's money supply raises its price level by 10%.
Statement II: In the long run, a permanent increase in the money supply lowers the interest rate and raises real output.
Answer: B — Long run: real output depends only on factor endowments and technology, R is independent of Ms, and P adjusts proportionally so that M/P is unchanged.
Question 133
The US money supply increases permanently. Prices are sticky in the short run, and expectations adjust immediately. Which time path of E$/€ results?
Answer: C — This is exchange rate overshooting (Dornbusch, 1976): the R$ fall plus the higher Ee give a large initial depreciation; as P rises, M/P falls, R$ recovers and the dollar appreciates partially.
Question 134
Exchange rate overshooting after a permanent money supply increase would NOT occur if:
Answer: A — Sticky prices are crucial: with flexible prices M/P and R$ do not change, and E jumps straight to its new long-run level.
Question 135
Closed economy, IS-LM and AS-AD framework. The money supply increases permanently. Which statement is correct?
Answer: C — LM shifts down, investment rises and AD shifts right with P fixed (menu costs). Workers then demand higher wages, P rises, M/P falls, and LM shifts back.
Question 136
Suppose that, contrary to the basic model, a money supply increase also raises real output in the short run. Compared with the basic overshooting model, the short-run depreciation of the currency will be:
Answer: B — The exchange rate could only undershoot if the interest rate actually rose when the money supply increased.
Question 137
E$/€ = 1.20 (dollars per euro) and E¥/$ = 150 (yen per dollar). By triangular arbitrage, the yen-euro rate E¥/€ must be:
Answer: D — 1 euro = 1.20 dollars = 1.20 × 150 = 180 yen.
Question 138
The velocity of money is V = Y / (M/P), and in equilibrium M/P = L(R, Y). Holding Y constant, an increase in the interest rate R:
Answer: C — Higher R lowers L(R, Y), so Y/L rises.
Question 139
Which of the following is NOT a reason why the law of one price may fail?
Answer: D — LOP requires costless trade and perfect competition; arbitrage then equalizes prices expressed in a common currency.
Question 140
A representative basket of goods costs 150 euros in the Netherlands and 180 Swiss francs in Switzerland. If absolute PPP holds, the exchange rate is:
Answer: D — E(EUR/CHF) = PNL / PCH = 150/180 = 5/6, so 180 francs buy the basket in CH and 180 × 5/6 = 150 euros buy it in NL.
Question 141
Expected inflation is 6% in the US and 2% in the euro area. According to relative PPP, over the year E$/€:
Answer: B — Relative PPP: %change in E$/€ = piUS - piEU = 6% - 2% = 4%.
Question 142
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: Absolute PPP implies relative PPP.
Statement II: Relative PPP implies absolute PPP.
Answer: B — If price levels are equal in a common currency at all times, their changes are equal too; but equal changes can coexist with a constant level gap.
Question 143
A Big Mac costs 5.50 dollars in the US and 440 yen in Japan. The actual exchange rate is 160 yen per dollar. The Big Mac 'implied' PPP exchange rate and the valuation of the yen are:
Answer: D — Implied rate = 440/5.50 = 80. Actual 160 yen per dollar means the yen is weaker than PPP implies: 1 - 80/160 = 50% undervaluation.
Question 144
Evaluate the two statements below about the monetary approach to exchange rates (prices fully flexible, absolute PPP holds, interest rates and output exogenous).
Statement I: An exogenous rise in the European interest rate causes the euro to depreciate against the dollar.
Statement II: In the short-run asset market model with sticky prices and given expectations, a rise in the euro interest rate causes the euro to appreciate against the dollar.
Answer: A — Monetary approach: higher REU lowers L(REU, YEU), so PEU must rise to clear the money market, and via PPP the euro depreciates. Short run: euro deposits become more attractive, so the euro appreciates.
Question 145
In the monetary approach, fill in the blanks: a permanent 10% increase in the European money supply makes the euro [1] by [2] against the dollar, while a rise in European real output makes the euro [3].
Answer: D — M up 10% means PEU up 10%, and via PPP the euro depreciates 10%. Higher Y raises money demand, so PEU must fall, and the euro appreciates.
Question 146
In the monetary approach with flexible exchange rates, the (log) exchange rate is s = m - mf + i - if - (y - yf), where s is the price of foreign currency in home currency and the subscript f denotes Foreign. Which statement is correct?
Answer: C — s falls (Home appreciates) when mf grows faster; s rises (Home depreciates) with yf because of the +yf term. Higher y lowers s; higher i raises s.
Question 147
Expected US inflation rises permanently from 2% to 5%, while expected euro-area inflation is unchanged. According to the Fisher effect, in the long run:
Answer: D — Interest parity + relative PPP give RUS - REU = πeUS - πeEU, so a 3-point rise in expected inflation raises RUS one-for-one.
Question 148
In the long-run model with flexible prices and PPP, expected US inflation rises unexpectedly. Which sequence is correct?
Answer: D — This contrasts with the sticky-price model, where a higher interest rate appreciates the currency. The difference comes from what causes the interest rate to change.
Question 149
E$/€ = 1.10. A reference basket costs 200 euros in the euro area and 250 dollars in the US. The real exchange rate q$/€ = (E$/€ x PEU) / PUS is:
Answer: A — q = 1.10 × 200 / 250 = 220/250 = 0.88 US baskets per EU basket. A rise in q is a real depreciation of the dollar.
Question 150
Which of the following causes a REAL depreciation of the US dollar against the euro?
Answer: A — More relative supply of US goods lowers their relative price, so q rises. Higher relative demand for US goods (or lower demand for EU goods) is a real appreciation; money has no real effect.
Question 151
Under the real exchange rate approach, a permanent 10% increase in the US money supply leads in the long run to:
Answer: D — Monetary factors change only nominal variables: PUS rises 10% and E$/€ rises 10%, leaving q unchanged.
Question 152
The nominal interest rate is 5% and expected inflation is 7%. The approximate expected real interest rate is:
Answer: B — re is approximately R - πe = 5% - 7% = -2% (exact: 1.05/1.07 - 1, about -1.9%; the approximation is good when R and π are small).
Question 153
Investors expect the US dollar to depreciate in real terms against the euro by 2% per year. The expected real interest rate in the euro area is 1%. According to real interest parity, the expected real interest rate in the US is:
Answer: D — reUS - reEU = (qe - q)/q = 2%: investors need a higher US real return to compensate for the expected real depreciation.
Question 154
RUS - REU = 4 percentage points. Expected inflation is 3% in the US and 1% in the euro area. The implied expected real depreciation of the dollar against the euro is:
Answer: B — RUS - REU = expected real depreciation of the dollar + (πeUS - πeEU): 4% = x + 2%, so x = 2%. Only when absolute PPP holds (q constant) is this the Fisher equation.
Question 155
Evaluate the two statements below.
Statement I: Long-run cross-country data (1960-2001) show that countries with high inflation relative to the US depreciated against the dollar roughly in proportion, which supports relative PPP in the long run.
Statement II: This evidence implies that the law of one price holds for every individual good.
Answer: B — Relative PPP for price indices does not imply LOP for each good; Big Mac and car prices show large LOP violations (and absolute PPP finds no support).
Question 156
'Pricing to market', one reason why PPP fails, refers to:
Answer: D — Other reasons PPP fails: trade barriers and non-traded goods (transport costs, services), and different preferences/baskets across countries.
